Interest in mahjong has grown a lot in recent years, and many people who love the game are wondering whether they could teach it. The good news: you don't need a storefront or a big budget to start. You need a clear offer, a place to play, a fair price and a way for people to find you.

Step 1: Pick your style and your students

Decide which version you'll teach. In the US, American mah jongg — played with jokers, the Charleston and a new card each year from the National Mah Jongg League — is the most common. Hong Kong and Riichi (Japanese) mahjong have their own followings, and there are many regional styles too. Teach what you know best.

Then decide who you're teaching. Complete beginners? Players who know the basics and want to improve? Groups of friends who want a fun afternoon? Each needs a slightly different class.

Step 2: Choose your formats

Most teachers mix a few of these:

  • Beginner series: a set of sessions (often three or four) that take someone from zero to playing a full game.
  • Drop-in practice or open play: a relaxed session for people who have learned and want to keep playing.
  • Private lessons: one-to-one or one household, usually priced higher.
  • Parties and events: birthdays, bridal showers, corporate team events — often your best-paying bookings.
  • Online lessons: over video, for students who aren't near you.

Step 3: Set your prices

Prices vary a lot by city, so look at what other teachers and local activity classes charge near you. A few principles help:

  • Price per person for group classes, and per session or per hour for private lessons and events.
  • Sell beginner series as a package — it fills seats in advance and students are more likely to finish.
  • Charge more for events where you bring sets, travel, or style the table.
  • Take payment upfront when people book, so no-shows don't cost you.

Quick check: add up your costs for a class (venue, snacks, your travel, wear on your sets) and make sure four students cover them with room to spare. If they don't, raise the price or find a cheaper venue.

Step 4: Find a place to play

You can start at home, but a regular venue makes it easier for strangers to say yes. Ask cafés, wine bars, libraries, community centres and clubhouses — many are happy to host a quieter weekday afternoon that brings in customers. Agree the basics up front: how many tables, how long, and whether players are expected to buy food or drinks.

Step 5: Get the equipment

For American mahjong, each table needs a set with jokers, four racks, and a current card for every player. For classes, it's worth having a couple of spare sets and printed cheat-sheets for beginners. Clear, easy-to-read tiles matter more than fancy ones when people are learning.

Step 6: Sort the practical side

Check what your area needs for a small business — for example, registering the business, keeping records for tax, and whether your venue requires insurance. A simple booking system with online payment will save you a lot of messaging back and forth.

Step 7: Get your first students

  • Start with your circle: friends, neighbours and their friends. Ask everyone who comes to bring one person next time.
  • Set up Instagram and Facebook properly — a clear bio, a booking link, and a few posts showing your table. Our guide to filling your classes with Instagram walks through it.
  • Partner with your venue — ask them to share your class on their channels.
  • Collect reviews after every class and share them.
  • Keep in touch — collect emails so you can tell past students when new classes open.

Step 8: Keep them coming back

Your best students are the ones who've already been. After a beginner series, invite them to open play, a next-level class, or a friendly tournament. A small group of regulars gives you steady income and brings in their friends.
